\begin{abstract}
Knowledge graphs give a language model something it does not have on its own: named entities, typed relations, and a source for each claim. That makes them an obvious partner for medical language models, which are fluent but hard to audit and easy to mislead. A large literature now combines the two. Much of it treats the presence of a graph as proof that the model is grounded, and reports answer accuracy without checking whether the right part of the graph was retrieved, whether the model used it, or whether the result would be safe in a clinic. This survey asks instead when the graph earns its place. We follow a small set of representative systems through the path they have to survive: building and updating the graph, indexing it, retrieving from it, reasoning over what comes back, verifying the output, and watching the system once people depend on it. We classify methods by where the graph and the model touch and how tightly they are joined, because pasting triples into a prompt and training a graph-aware encoder are not the same intervention. We map the graphs, ontologies, clinical datasets, and benchmarks this work runs on, and ask what each one enables for retrieval and for tool-using agents. The answer is conditional. Graphs help when they hold knowledge the model lacks, when entity linking works, when retrieval returns a small and relevant subgraph, and when the comparison is a matched text-retrieval baseline. They help less, and sometimes hurt, when coverage is thin, mappings are wrong, or retrieved context crowds out the decisive evidence. Recent results on weak citation support, poor handling of time, and snippets that help one model and confuse another all point the same way. We close with the failure modes that matter in practice, including one peculiar to closed-loop systems: a model proposal written back into the graph, then retrieved later as if it were independent evidence.
\end{abstract}
